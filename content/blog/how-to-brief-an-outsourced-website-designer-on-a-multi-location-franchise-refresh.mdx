## Why a multi-location refresh needs a different brief

A franchise website refresh fails in predictable ways when the brief treats every location page as a copy of the home page. The vendor designs a handsome master template, then discovers that forty operators expect their own hours, offers, and staff photos. Rework starts before the first review, and the brand team loses confidence in the schedule. A brief that names the page types, the content owners, and the approval path prevents most of that churn.

The brief should also state what the refresh is not allowed to change. Some franchise systems guard the logo, typography, and navigation structure across every market. Others allow local colour and campaign banners within a defined frame. Writing those boundaries down gives the designer a stable rule instead of a series of one-off arguments. It also gives the franchisee a clear reason when a request is declined.

## Inventory the location page templates

Start by listing every template that can appear under a location URL. A typical set includes the location landing page, a services or menu page, a contact page, and a booking or enquiry confirmation. Some systems also publish a local offer page, a team page, and a careers page. The brief should name each template, its required fields, and whether it is shared across locations or unique to one.

For each template, record the modules that may vary and the modules that must stay fixed. A hero image might be local, while the primary call to action stays brand-controlled. Opening hours may vary, but the privacy notice and legal footer should not. This module map becomes the design system for the refresh and the acceptance checklist for each location. Without it, the vendor cannot tell a valid local variation from an exception.

## Draw the line between brand and local content

Draw an explicit line between content the brand publishes and content a franchisee may edit. Brand copy usually covers the value proposition, service descriptions, guarantees, and legal text. Local content usually covers addresses, hours, named staff, community involvement, and approved offers. Put each content type in one column with an owner and an approval rule. The designer can then build fields that match the governance instead of inventing it later.

The line also determines how much freedom the CMS should offer. If franchisees can rewrite service descriptions, the brand loses consistency and the support team inherits a review burden. If they cannot change their own opening hours, the site becomes inaccurate on the busiest days. A useful brief describes both extremes and chooses a middle setting deliberately. That choice should be visible in the page template and the editor permissions.

## Settle photography and image rights

Photography is the most common source of rework in a franchise refresh. The brand may supply a library of approved interiors, products, and people, while each operator wants to show the actual local team. The brief should say which slots accept local uploads, what crop and resolution are required, and who checks image rights. It should also state whether the vendor is expected to retouch, resize, or reject unsuitable files.

Image rights deserve a named owner because a local photo can create a legal problem for the whole system. Ask franchisees to confirm they have permission from every person who is identifiable and from the photographer. Store that confirmation beside the asset rather than in an email thread. The brief can require a simple rights field in the CMS and a review step before publication. That small control protects the brand and gives the designer a clear handoff.

## Design the franchisee review loop

Design the review loop around the people who actually approve local pages. A brand marketing lead may approve the template, a regional manager may approve the content, and a franchisee may approve the local facts. The brief should name each approver, the order of review, and the response window. It should also say what happens when an approver misses the window, so the schedule does not stall silently.

Give reviewers a page they can judge without guessing. A useful review link shows the location page with realistic content, the mobile layout, and the editable fields marked. The reviewer should be able to compare the page with the brand standard and see which parts are locked. Collect decisions in one place with a clear accept, change, or reject choice. A vendor that receives scattered comments across email and chat will rebuild the same section twice.

## Agree on the rollout order and fallback

Set the rollout order before design begins. A pilot group of five to ten locations can expose missing fields, bad photography, and confusing approval rules without risking the whole network. The brief should name the pilot group, the success checks, and the date when the remaining locations are released. It should also define what counts as a blocking defect and who can pause the rollout.

Plan the fallback for a location that is not ready. The old page may need to stay live, redirect to the parent brand page, or publish with a clear holding message. Each option has a different effect on search results and customer trust. The brief should choose one default and require an owner for every exception. A refresh that has no fallback turns a single late franchisee into a network-wide launch risk.

## Keep the brief short enough to use

A good franchise brief is shorter than the design system it describes. It names the templates, the content owners, the image rules, the review path, and the rollout order. It also states the decisions the vendor is not allowed to make alone. That clarity lets an outsourced designer spend time on the pages instead of chasing missing answers. The result is a refresh that looks consistent without erasing the local character that makes each location worth visiting.
