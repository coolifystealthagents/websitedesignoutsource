# A Stakeholder Interview Plan for Outsourced Website Design

## Use interviews to reduce decision risk

Stakeholder interviews are not a ceremonial kickoff activity. They should uncover information that changes the website brief: which audiences matter, what visitors must accomplish, which claims require proof, where operations constrain the experience, and who can approve a tradeoff. An outsourced design team needs this context, but a long series of unfocused conversations can create more ambiguity than it removes.

Begin with a short decision statement for the research. Examples include selecting the primary conversion path, resolving competing navigation priorities, or understanding why qualified prospects hesitate. List the artifacts the interviews must inform. This prevents the discussion from becoming a general tour of each department and gives participants a clear reason to contribute.

## Choose participants by knowledge and authority

Map the perspectives needed rather than inviting only senior leaders. Sales may know common objections, support may know recurring failures, operations may own fulfillment constraints, and legal may know which promises require qualification. Include the person accountable for the website outcome and people close to customers. When relevant, distinguish people who supply evidence from those who make the final decision.

Avoid treating one articulate participant as representative of an entire audience. Record what each person directly observes, what they infer, and what they prefer. If customer insight is needed, stakeholder opinion cannot substitute for customer research. The plan should state where internal interviews end and user interviews, analytics, search data, or support records begin.

## Prepare an evidence request

Send a concise request before each session. Ask for examples such as sales call notes, approved claims, support categories, workflow diagrams, policy documents, analytics reports, or current-page feedback. Tell participants not to forward sensitive customer information into an unsuitable workspace. Evidence lets the designer test statements that otherwise sound equally confident.

Review available material and customize a small portion of the guide. A support leader should not receive exactly the same prompts as a finance approver. Keep a shared core, however, so answers can be compared across interviews. Useful core questions ask about audience priorities, desired actions, proof, failure modes, constraints, and approval.

## Ask about behavior before solutions

Questions such as “What should the homepage look like?” invite premature solutions. Ask what prospective customers are trying to learn, where they get stuck, what they misunderstand, and what evidence changes their mind. Request a recent example: “Walk me through the last qualified prospect who delayed a decision.” Concrete episodes expose vocabulary and operational details that abstract opinions miss.

Probe recommendations without arguing. If someone asks for a carousel, ask which content needs priority, why simultaneous visibility matters, and how success would be measured. The underlying need may be executive visibility, frequent promotion, or fear of removing content. The design team can then evaluate alternatives against that need instead of accepting a component prescription.

## Capture disagreements explicitly

Do not average conflicting views into a vague summary. Create a decision log with the disputed question, each position, supporting evidence, effect on users, decision owner, and deadline. Distinguish factual conflicts from value judgments. Analytics might resolve which pages attract qualified traffic, while only an accountable leader can choose between two strategic audiences.

During synthesis, use confidence labels. A policy document can confirm a constraint; one employee's recollection is a lead to investigate. Mark assumptions that require validation and identify the cheapest suitable method. This protects the outsourced team from presenting consensus that does not exist and gives the client a manageable escalation list.

## Make sessions accessible and candid

Share the purpose, duration, recording policy, and discussion areas in advance. Offer the guide in an accessible format and allow written follow-up. Ask permission before recording. Some participants will speak more openly one-to-one than in a mixed-seniority workshop, particularly when discussing failed processes or approval bottlenecks.

Use a facilitator and a note owner when possible. The facilitator listens and probes; the note owner tags evidence, decisions, terms, and follow-ups. Avoid publishing attributed criticism beyond the agreed audience. The goal is not to score departments. It is to reveal conditions the website must support.

## Synthesize into design inputs

Produce a brief synthesis soon after the interviews. It should include audience jobs, priority journeys, claims and proof, content gaps, operational constraints, vocabulary, measurement needs, open questions, and decisions required. Link conclusions back to anonymized notes or approved evidence so reviewers can challenge the reasoning.

Translate findings into testable requirements. “Users need confidence” is too broad; “the service page must explain the review stages and identify what the client approves at each one” can be designed and tested. Keep interesting observations out of the backlog unless they affect scope, content, interaction, measurement, or risk.

## Set an acceptance checkpoint

Before visual exploration expands, ask the decision owner to review the synthesis, unresolved conflicts, and proposed implications. Approval should mean the team accepts these as the current inputs, not that every participant received a preferred feature. Record corrections and the date of the accepted version.

The handoff should include the participant map, interview guide, consent and recording rules, notes location, evidence index, synthesis, open-decision log, and owners. This package makes discovery auditable without turning private conversations into public copy. It also gives future team members a path back to the reasoning behind the site.

## Know when to reopen discovery

Revisit interviews when strategy, audience, regulation, service delivery, or decision ownership changes materially. Do not repeat the entire exercise because one stakeholder joins late. Give that person the accepted synthesis, ask what evidence it misses, and route true changes through the decision log.

A disciplined interview plan respects participants' time and the design team's need for usable inputs. Its value is not the number of calls completed. Its value is fewer hidden constraints, clearer tradeoffs, and website decisions grounded in evidence the client can recognize.
