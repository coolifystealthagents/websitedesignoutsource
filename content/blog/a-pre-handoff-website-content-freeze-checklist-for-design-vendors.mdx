## Why a freeze window belongs before handoff, not after launch

Most content freezes are written for launch week, when the site is already built and the vendor is checking links. That timing is too late for the handoff from design to build, because the build team needs a stable set of strings, labels, and page structures to implement. If copy keeps arriving after the handoff, the vendor ends up rebuilding components that were signed off days earlier. A short freeze before handoff protects the design decisions that the build depends on.

The freeze also gives the client a clear moment to stop editing. Without one, a stakeholder can reopen a headline on the morning the developer starts, and nobody can tell whether the change is a correction or a new direction. A named window turns that ambiguity into a date and a rule. It does not stop the business from changing its mind, but it makes the cost of a late change visible to everyone.

## Set the window and write down both dates

Choose a freeze start and a handoff date, then publish both in the project calendar. A common pattern is three business days of freeze before the handoff, with the final approval due at noon on the last day. The window should be long enough to resolve a genuine correction and short enough that the client does not lose momentum. Write the dates in the project plan, the review invite, and the handoff note so no one can claim they missed them.

State the time zone and the exact cutoff, not just the day. "Copy freezes Friday" is ambiguous when the client and vendor sit in different regions. A precise timestamp removes the argument about whether a message sent at 6 p.m. arrived in time. It also lets the vendor schedule the final review without waiting for a late email that may never come.

## Name one owner for each content lane

A freeze fails when every stakeholder believes someone else is collecting the final copy. Assign one owner for each lane, such as marketing pages, product descriptions, legal text, and support content. Each owner confirms that their lane is complete and that no promised copy is still sitting in a document or a chat thread. The vendor should have a single contact who can resolve conflicts between owners.

The owner is not necessarily the most senior person. It is the person who knows where the copy lives and can say whether the latest version has been approved. Record the owner's name and role beside the lane, not only in a meeting note. When a late change appears, the vendor then knows exactly whom to ask for a decision instead of broadcasting the question to the whole project.

## Collect evidence that the copy is final

A verbal "looks good" is not evidence that survives a handoff. Ask each owner for a dated approval that names the page or section and the version they approved. The approval can live in the ticket, a signed review sheet, or a comment on the prototype, as long as it is findable after the project closes. The vendor should store it with the handoff pack rather than in a personal inbox.

The evidence should also show what was approved, not only that something was. Attach the final copy file, the page list, or a link to the approved prototype snapshot. If the copy changes after approval, the old approval no longer covers the new text. A short version label and a date make that distinction obvious and prevent a stale sign-off from being used to justify a later edit.

## Define what may still change during the freeze

A freeze with no exceptions will be broken by a legal correction or a pricing error. Write down the narrow categories that can still change, such as a factual error, a compliance requirement, or a broken call to action. Each exception needs an owner, a reason, and a record in the exception log. The vendor should also state who can approve an exception and how quickly the build team will be told.

Everything outside those categories waits for the next release. That rule protects the build schedule and gives the client a predictable answer when a stakeholder asks for a last-minute rewrite. It also keeps the exception log useful, because it contains real exceptions rather than every routine edit. Review the log at the handoff meeting so the build team knows exactly which items are still moving.

## Write the rollback rule before the handoff

Decide what happens if the final copy is not approved by the cutoff. The default should be simple: the build proceeds with the last approved version, and the late copy moves to the first post-handoff release. Name the person who can trigger that default and the person who must be told. Without a default, the project stalls while everyone waits for a decision that no one has authority to make.

The rollback rule should also cover a copy error found after the handoff. If the error affects legal text or a primary action, the team may need to pause the affected component rather than ship it. Define who can pause, what evidence is required, and how the pause is recorded. A rollback that is agreed in advance is a control; one invented during an incident is usually a negotiation.

## Close the freeze with a short, repeatable review

Hold one review at the cutoff with the lane owners, the design lead, and the build lead. Walk through the page list, confirm each approval, and read the exception log aloud. The output is a short handoff note that names the frozen version, the open exceptions, and the rollback owner. Keep the note to one page so people actually read it.

After the handoff, check a small sample of built pages against the frozen copy. Look for truncated headings, missing labels, and text that was quietly rewritten during implementation. Record any mismatch and route it back to the owner rather than fixing it silently. That final check turns the freeze from a calendar event into evidence that the handoff was real.
