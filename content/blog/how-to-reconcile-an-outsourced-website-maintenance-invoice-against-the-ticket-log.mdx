## Why the invoice needs a second record

A maintenance invoice is a claim about time, and the ticket log is the only record that can test it. When the two documents live in different systems, the client pays whatever the invoice says and hopes the work happened as described. A monthly reconciliation turns that hope into a short, repeatable check. It also gives the vendor a fair chance to explain a line before anyone disputes it.

The reconciliation is not an accusation. Most mismatches come from naming, rounding, or a ticket that was closed under a different project code. The goal is a shared record that both sides can read without reconstructing the month from memory. Treat the first pass as a translation exercise, and only escalate after the same gap appears twice.

## Build a ticket ledger before the month closes

Export the ticket list on the last working day, not after the invoice arrives. The export should include the ticket id, the request date, the close date, the logged hours, the person who did the work, and the status. Ask for the same fields every month so the columns stay comparable. A stable export is easier to audit than a fresh report built to answer a complaint.

Store the export beside the invoice with the billing period in the filename. Do not edit the vendor's file; keep a working copy and record your own notes in a separate column. If the vendor uses a portal, download the csv rather than copying rows by hand. The original file is the evidence, and the working copy is where the reconciliation happens.

## Reconcile hours line by line

Match each invoice line to one or more ticket ids before you look at the total. A line that covers several tickets should list them, or at least the date range and the project code. When the invoice groups work by theme, ask for the underlying ticket list rather than accepting the summary. The theme is useful for the narrative but useless for checking hours.

Add the logged hours for the matched tickets and compare the sum with the billed hours. Record the difference in absolute hours and as a share of the line, because a two-hour gap means something different on a ten-hour line than on a sixty-hour one. Note every line that cannot be matched, even when the amount is small. An unmatched line is a question, not yet a finding.

## Separate included work from billable change

The retainer covers a defined set of work, and the invoice may mix included hours with approved change work. Sort each line into one of three buckets: included maintenance, approved change, and unclassified. The unclassified bucket is where disputes start, so keep it visible in the summary. Do not let a change order hide inside a routine maintenance line.

Check that every change line cites an approved request and a quoted amount. If the work began before approval, record the date the approval arrived and who gave it. This is the same boundary the [retainer scope guide](/blog/how-to-set-a-maintenance-retainer-boundary-between-fixes-and-new-features) describes, applied to the invoice rather than the request. A change line without a matching approval is the clearest signal to query.

## Investigate the three common mismatches

The first mismatch is a ticket closed in one month and billed in the next. Check the close date rather than the request date, then decide which period owns the hours. The second is rounding, where short tasks are billed in fifteen-minute blocks and the total drifts upward. Ask for the rounding rule in writing and test it on a sample of ten tickets.

The third is duplicate billing, where a ticket appears on two lines because the work was split across categories. Search the invoice for repeated ticket ids before you search for repeated amounts. Duplicates often survive because the totals differ after rounding. When you find one, report the ticket id and both line positions so the vendor can correct the record quickly.

## Turn the reconciliation into a monthly one-pager

Write a one-page summary with four numbers: tickets billed, hours billed, hours matched, and hours queried. Add the name of the person who reviewed the invoice and the date the review closed. Keep the detail in the workbook and the summary in the message, so the reader can choose the depth. A short summary gets read; a forty-row spreadsheet usually does not.

Send the summary before the payment run and give the vendor a stated window to respond. Name the contact who will answer questions and the date by which a corrected invoice must arrive. This mirrors the [monthly maintenance report audit](/blog/a-monthly-maintenance-report-audit-for-outsourced-website-clients), where the report and the evidence are reviewed together. A predictable window removes the urgency that turns a query into a fight.

## Escalate patterns, not single line items

One unmatched line can be a clerical slip, and treating it as fraud wastes goodwill. Track queries across three months and look for a pattern by vendor, by ticket type, or by person. A repeated gap in the same category is a process problem that deserves a written fix. The pattern, not the single line, is what you take to the account review.

When a pattern appears, ask for a corrective action with a date and an owner. For example, the vendor might add ticket ids to every invoice line or stop billing rounded blocks for tasks under ten minutes. Record the agreed change in the next month's summary. Follow-up is what separates a reconciliation habit from a one-time complaint.

## Keep the evidence for renewal

At renewal, the reconciliation history answers questions that a proposal cannot. It shows how often the invoice needed correction, which categories caused the most queries, and whether the vendor fixed the process. That record is more useful than a satisfaction score because it comes from documents both sides already accepted. Use it to set the hour cap and the review cadence for the next term.

Archive each month's export, invoice, and summary in one folder with a simple index. Keep the files for the full contract term and for the period your finance team requires. Written change control matters in public contracting too, and [FAR Part 43](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/part-43) treats a modification as a formal record rather than a conversation. A clean archive makes the next renewal a review of evidence instead of a debate about memory.
