# Build a Claims Approval Workflow Into an Outsourced Website Project

## Identify claims as content dependencies

In regulated or evidence-sensitive work, a claim is not merely a sentence. It depends on a source, scope, audience, date, qualification, and approval. The web team needs to know which content carries that dependency before layout and components are finalized. Otherwise a crucial qualifier may be reduced to unreadable fine print or separated from the statement it explains.

Create a claim register during discovery. Assign each proposed statement a stable ID, category, exact wording, evidence location, evidence owner, applicable market, required qualifier, approving role, status, and review trigger. Include visual claims created by charts, icons, before-and-after images, rankings, and comparison tables. An implication can create the same reader expectation as a headline.

## Keep three versions visible

Maintain the author draft, approved wording, and published rendering as connected but distinct records. Reviewers need to see the exact context where a claim will appear, including heading, nearby image, call to action, footnote, and responsive layout. Approval of a spreadsheet phrase does not automatically approve a mobile card that truncates its qualifier.

Locking all copy too early can block useful design work, while designing with invented text creates hidden risk. Use clearly marked provisional content and prevent it from reaching production. The source of truth should expose approval status to editors and release checks. Never use a colour alone to distinguish approved from pending material.

## Design components that preserve meaning

Specify relationships between a claim and its qualification. A reference link, disclosure, or condition must remain discoverable at supported widths, zoom levels, and text sizes. Accordions should not conceal information a user needs to understand the main promise. Charts require accessible names, data context, and an alternative that preserves the relevant conclusion without overstating precision.

Reusable claim components can store source ID, market, approval date, and review date privately while rendering only appropriate public information. Do not expose internal legal notes or confidential evidence. Test copied text, print styles, social previews, search snippets, and structured data where they could repeat a claim outside its intended context.

## Route changes by risk

Define who may correct spelling, who may alter approved meaning, and who handles urgent removal. A routine editor should not be able to rewrite a regulated statement merely because the CMS field is editable. Conversely, an expired claim should not remain public for days while everyone waits for the original designer. Provide a company-controlled disable or replacement path with an audit trail.

Changes to evidence, product capability, market availability, guidance, or campaign terms should trigger review. Record affected routes and components so owners can find every occurrence. Search alone is not sufficient when the same idea appears as an image, metadata, or generated snippet.

## Test the workflow with an uncomfortable scenario

Choose a claim whose evidence expires or whose scope changes. Ask the content owner to identify every public occurrence, replace or remove it, obtain the required approval, preview responsive contexts, publish, and confirm the result. Then inspect caches, feeds, search metadata, and scheduled campaigns that may preserve the earlier statement. The exercise reveals whether the workflow works under time pressure.

Record what cannot be withdrawn immediately, such as third-party caches or printed material, and assign the communication decision. Do not promise instant deletion everywhere. The goal is controlled, observable response with a clear record of what changed and why.

## Use authoritative guidance without outsourcing judgment

Rules differ by sector and geography. The company should obtain appropriate legal or compliance advice rather than asking a general-purpose design vendor to interpret the law. For advertising in the United States, the Federal Trade Commission's [Advertising and Marketing Basics](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing) is an authoritative starting point; it is not a universal approval checklist or legal opinion for a particular claim.

The accepted handoff includes the claim register, roles, source and approval locations, component behavior, CMS permissions, expiry process, emergency removal route, and release evidence. This lets the outside team produce clear pages while responsibility for truth remains with the organization that makes the claim.

## Build release evidence around claim identity

At release time, generate or record the set of claim IDs included in affected routes, their approved revisions, and the production release identity. A reviewer should be able to move from a public statement to its current approval without searching old email threads. If the CMS cannot enforce this relationship automatically, use a proportionate release checklist and periodic sample rather than pretending the gap does not exist.

Include non-page surfaces in sampling: page titles, descriptions, structured data, downloadable files, images containing text, banners, email-linked landing states, and cached campaign variants. Compare the rendered wording and qualification to the approved record. A source file can be correct while a component clips or rearranges meaning in production.

When an exception is accepted, state its scope, risk owner, expiry, monitoring, and removal condition. Do not label it temporary without a date or event that forces reconsideration. The handoff reviewer should be able to find open exceptions and demonstrate urgent removal in a controlled environment. That proof is more valuable than a policy document that assumes every approval path will be followed perfectly. Schedule a periodic sample of live claims so drift, expired evidence, and broken qualification relationships are found before a complaint forces the review.
