# How to Verify Portfolio Evidence Before Hiring an Outsourced Website Team

## Treat a portfolio as a set of claims

A portfolio starts a useful conversation, but it is not proof by itself. A polished home page can conceal a weak content model, inaccessible controls, slow templates, or an implementation completed by somebody else. Before selecting an outsourced website design team, translate each attractive example into a claim you can test. If the vendor says it solved a navigation problem, ask what changed, who approved it, and how the result was checked. If it presents an ecommerce redesign, distinguish visual design from product data, development, photography, and conversion work.

Begin with your own risk profile. A marketing site with ten stable pages needs different evidence from a multilingual catalog with regional editors. List the three to five capabilities that would make your project fail if they were missing. Those might include information architecture, accessible interaction design, CMS modeling, performance work, migration planning, or stakeholder facilitation. Review portfolio items against that list, not against a general impression of taste.

## Confirm the team's actual contribution

Ask the vendor to describe its responsibility in plain terms. Useful answers identify the problem, starting constraints, deliverables, collaborators, approval path, and the part the team personally produced. A studio may legitimately show work completed alongside a brand agency, internal engineering group, or another contractor. The important point is accurate attribution. A vague statement such as "we helped transform the experience" does not tell you whether the proposed team can perform your scope.

Request one artifact that demonstrates the claimed contribution without exposing a former client's confidential information. Examples include a redacted page inventory, component specification, responsive annotation, usability finding, or acceptance checklist. You do not need another company's private analytics. You need enough process evidence to see how the team turns an ambiguous request into a reviewable decision. If no artifact can be shared, ask the vendor to walk through a fictional example using the same method.

## Inspect the live result carefully

When a portfolio links to a public site, remember that the site may have changed since delivery. Record the review date and ask which routes or components still represent the vendor's work. Check the experience at narrow and wide viewports, use keyboard navigation, enlarge text, and visit a deeper content page. A screenshot proves only that one composition existed. A live review can reveal whether navigation, headings, forms, focus states, error recovery, and content templates behave coherently.

Do not infer commercial results from design alone. Traffic, offer strength, pricing, advertising, sales operations, and seasonality all influence conversion. If a case study states a measurable outcome, ask for the measurement window, baseline, metric definition, and other changes during that period. Treat an unverified percentage as marketing copy rather than selection evidence. The United States Federal Trade Commission's [Advertising FAQs for small businesses](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business) are a useful reminder that objective claims need a reasonable basis.

## Compare relevance, not surface similarity

A vendor does not need to have designed a website for a business identical to yours. In fact, a gallery of nearly identical sites may indicate a narrow template rather than adaptable judgment. Look for comparable constraints: a regulated approval process, complex service explanation, multiple audiences, legacy content, distributed editors, or an important lead handoff. Ask how lessons transfer and which assumptions would need fresh research.

Create a compact evidence matrix. Put project risks in rows and portfolio examples in columns. For each cell, record direct evidence, indirect evidence, or no evidence. Add notes about who performed the work and whether the artifact is current. This prevents one impressive project from overshadowing a gap that affects your delivery. It also makes shortlist discussions more specific than competing preferences about visual style.

## Use references to test working behavior

References are most useful when questions concern observable behavior. Ask a former client how the team handled missing content, conflicting feedback, late technical constraints, and decisions that changed cost or timing. Ask whether files and accounts were handed over cleanly, whether acceptance criteria were clear, and what the client would prepare differently next time. Avoid fishing for confidential details or asking only whether the client was happy.

Confirm that the reference relates to comparable work and, if possible, to people who will join your project. Agencies evolve, and a strong engagement from several years ago may not describe the proposed delivery team. If direct references are unavailable, use a paid discovery exercise to observe communication and reasoning before committing to a large build. The exercise should produce a useful artifact you retain, not speculative design created without context.

## Score evidence before the pitch meeting

Define the scoring method before presentations. A simple scale can distinguish unsupported assertion, plausible explanation, reviewable artifact, and verified outcome. Weight capabilities according to project risk. Require reviewers to cite the portfolio item or answer behind each score. This reduces the chance that confidence, production quality, or personal chemistry quietly substitutes for delivery evidence.

Give vendors the same opportunity to explain gaps. A team may have deep capability but lack permission to display relevant client work. A structured sample, redacted artifact, or scenario walkthrough can supply fair alternative evidence. Record exceptions explicitly so they do not become invisible favors. Procurement should reward candid boundaries as well as strong examples, because a vendor that names what it did not do is easier to govern after kickoff.

## Make verification part of the contract handoff

Carry validated claims into scope and acceptance language. If the chosen team demonstrated a component documentation method, state which components require documentation. If it showed a migration reconciliation process, identify inventories and signoffs. The goal is not to force a past solution onto a new project. It is to connect selection evidence to concrete obligations, owners, and review points.

Save the evidence matrix, attribution notes, reference findings, and unresolved risks with the vendor decision. Mark links that may change and retain permitted snapshots or redacted samples. This record helps a new stakeholder understand why the team was selected and prevents portfolio promises from being forgotten once delivery begins. It also gives both parties a fair foundation for the kickoff conversation.
