# Create a Component Deprecation Plan for an Outsourced Design System

Retire design-system components without silently breaking live pages, editor workflows, analytics, or accessibility. The useful deliverable is a decision record tied to a real customer task, not a generic checklist copied into a project folder. For this work, follow an editor or developer replacing an old shared component across active website routes. Write down the expected result, the evidence that will demonstrate it, and the person who owns recovery after the outside engagement ends.

The central failure to prevent is concrete: the component is removed before every usage is migrated, or two versions drift without a clear retirement owner. A polished screen or one successful demonstration does not prove the complete operating path. Acceptance must cover ownership, predictable variation, and recovery, while avoiding unsupported claims about security, compliance, customer results, or the business.

## Define the outcome before discussing implementation

Start at the route where the customer arrives. Record the action, the information already available, the decision the interface asks the person to make, and the final state that confirms completion. For design system component deprecation, this journey description keeps design, development, content, and operations aligned even if the chosen platform changes.

Separate company decisions from vendor proposals. The company should approve customer promises, policy, risk tolerance, data use, messages, and exception authority. The outsourced team may recommend an implementation and explain tradeoffs, but unknowns must remain visible. Turning an assumption into polished copy makes it harder for reviewers to see what still requires a decision.

Define evidence in observable terms. Name the route, starting state, input, expected result, actual result, environment, time, and reviewer. Include a normal path, a meaningful edge condition, and a safe recovery exercise. If production testing could cause harm, agree on a staging test and state exactly what remains unproven until release.

## Turn the record into topic-specific decisions

The component deprecation register must answer these questions against actual routes and representative examples:

1. **Component.** Capture component when the website changes state, then compare it with the customer-visible result.
2. **Current version.** Name who supplies and may alter current version, plus evidence that the recorded answer remains current.
3. **Affected routes.** Review affected routes against a real route and representative input; record any template, provider, account, or regional boundary.
4. **Replacement.** Make replacement recoverable so the company can find, explain, and restore a known-good value without an old vendor account.
5. **Behavior difference.** Capture behavior difference when the website changes state, then compare it with the customer-visible result.
6. **Content migration.** Name who supplies and may alter content migration, plus evidence that the recorded answer remains current.
7. **Analytics impact.** Review analytics impact against a real route and representative input; record any template, provider, account, or regional boundary.
8. **Accessibility check.** Make accessibility check recoverable so the company can find, explain, and restore a known-good value without an old vendor account.
9. **Deadline.** Capture deadline when the website changes state, then compare it with the customer-visible result.
10. **Exception owner.** Name who supplies and may alter exception owner, plus evidence that the recorded answer remains current.
11. **Removal proof.** Review removal proof against a real route and representative input; record any template, provider, account, or regional boundary.

Review the entries together. A technically correct value still fails acceptance when the customer message, support procedure, or approval authority contradicts it.

## Build a component deprecation register

Create one controlled component deprecation register rather than distributing decisions across chat, screenshots, and private vendor notes. Give every row a stable identifier and capture component, current version, affected routes, replacement, behavior difference, content migration, analytics impact, accessibility check, deadline, exception owner, and removal proof. Link to company-controlled evidence. Never place passwords, tokens, personal data, private logs, or recovery codes in a public article or broadly shared acceptance document.

Use statuses that communicate reality: proposed, ready for review, accepted, accepted with an exception, or blocked. An exception needs a reason, risk owner, review date, and removal condition. A bare label such as done cannot tell a future operator whether the item was implemented, tested, approved, or simply discussed.

Keep the current decision easy to locate while preserving history in version control or the company project system. When a row changes, record why, who approved it, which routes depend on it, and which checks must run again. That change trail matters for design system component deprecation because a small configuration edit can alter behavior beyond the page where it was first noticed.

## Model the states and transitions

Draw the important states as a short sequence, including who or what causes each transition. Label the point where the website hands responsibility to another service and the point where control returns. For every transition, decide what the customer sees, what the company records, and how an operator distinguishes delay from failure.

Do not collapse interface status, provider status, and business status into one word. A request may be accepted by the browser but rejected by an API. A provider may finish while the company record remains stale. The business may require review after the technical action succeeds. The component deprecation register should preserve these differences so retries and support messages do not create contradictory outcomes.

Choose retry and cancellation behavior deliberately. State whether another attempt is safe, whether input is preserved, whether the action can run twice, and when human review becomes necessary. Give customers useful next steps without exposing internal infrastructure or blaming a provider they did not choose.

## Test realistic variation and recovery

Run the expected path first with representative content and a clean session. Then cover CMS-stored component names, experiments, old landing pages, nested variants, third-party templates, visual snapshots, and rollback. The purpose is not to collect a huge screenshot archive. It is to test assumptions most likely to cause a customer-facing failure or leave the company unable to recover.

For each case, record expected and actual results separately. Save the release identity and time with the evidence. Check narrow and wide layouts, keyboard operation, focus order, readable labels, status announcements, reflow, and understandable errors wherever those qualities apply. A technical response alone does not demonstrate that the customer can understand or complete the task.

Exercise recovery without damaging production. Confirm what information survives, which action is safe to repeat, how an operator finds the relevant record, and which company-controlled account can correct the state. Where a real failure cannot be triggered safely, use lower-level evidence and document that limitation instead of claiming a broader pass.

## Run topic-specific acceptance scenarios

Adapt these cases to the actual platform without putting credentials or personal customer data into shared evidence:

- **CMS-stored component names:** Reproduce CMS-stored component names from a clean session and record both the interface response and underlying system state.
- **Experiments:** For experiments, decide what may be retained, what must be discarded, and which action is safe to repeat.
- **Old landing pages:** Simulate old landing pages safely; verify keyboard and narrow-screen recovery as well as the technical response.
- **Nested variants:** Use nested variants to test ownership: find the record, identify the alert recipient, correct the state, and prove recovery.
- **Third-party templates:** Reproduce third-party templates from a clean session and record both the interface response and underlying system state.
- **Visual snapshots:** For visual snapshots, decide what may be retained, what must be discarded, and which action is safe to repeat.
- **Rollback:** Simulate rollback safely; verify keyboard and narrow-screen recovery as well as the technical response.

Preserve expected and actual outcomes separately. A pass needs observable evidence and an owner; a surprise needs correction or a time-bounded exception with a retest trigger.

## Assign durable ownership and access

Name a company owner for approval, routine operation, and recovery. Distinguish decision authority from implementation access. A developer can implement design system component deprecation without authority to change company policy. An editor can perform a routine action without permission to change integration settings. A reviewer can accept visible behavior without becoming the recovery owner.

Record subscriptions, administrator accounts, billing relationships, data locations, notification destinations, source files, and renewal dates relevant to the decision. Use individual accounts and minimum necessary access where the platform permits. Verify that the company can change the configuration and obtain evidence without depending on a former vendor.

Plan offboarding during onboarding. Set expiry for temporary access, identify artifacts the company retains, test the recovery route, and remove permissions no longer required. If a new operator cannot locate the component deprecation register, explain the approval path, and perform one safe routine task, the handoff is incomplete even if the current page works.

## Use a proportional acceptance sequence

1. Confirm the company owner, customer task, affected routes, and dependencies for design system component deprecation.
2. Approve representative examples and every required field in the component deprecation register.
3. Review access, data handling, subscriptions, notification destinations, and recovery ownership.
4. Test one normal journey, two meaningful variations, and one safe failure or fallback case.
5. Check accessibility and content clarity for customer-visible controls, messages, and status changes.
6. Record release identity, environment, time, input, expected result, actual result, evidence, and reviewer.
7. Resolve defects or accept a time-bounded exception with a named owner and retest trigger.
8. Verify company-controlled administration and remove unnecessary vendor access.
9. Link the accepted record from the launch and maintenance handoff.
10. Schedule event-based review when platforms, routes, policies, providers, or owners change.

Expand the sample when a defect suggests a shared cause. A problem in a reusable component, common integration, customer-data path, or global configuration deserves review across representative routes. A narrowly scoped local defect should not automatically create a ceremonial full-site audit when the dependency record shows no wider effect.

## Plan change after launch

Set review triggers based on events rather than relying only on a calendar. Platform upgrades, new templates, provider changes, policy revisions, new regions, ownership changes, and customer reports can invalidate acceptance for design system component deprecation. For each trigger, name the record to update and the smallest meaningful evidence set to rerun.

Keep rollback practical. Record the last accepted state, the authority to restore it, customer communication needs, data consequences, and the test that proves restoration. A rollback instruction that depends on an unavailable vendor account is not a recovery plan. After a rollback, preserve the failed evidence long enough to diagnose the cause without retaining sensitive material unnecessarily.

Finally, review the record with someone who did not create it. Ask that person to find the current decision, describe a customer-facing failure, locate the owner, and explain the safe next action. This operator test often reveals missing context that visual review cannot.
