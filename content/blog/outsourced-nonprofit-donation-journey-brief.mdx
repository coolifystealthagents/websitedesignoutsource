# Brief a Nonprofit Donation Journey for an Outsourced Website Team

## Define the promise attached to the gift

A donation page joins an emotional decision to a financial and administrative process. Before designing it, state what the organization may promise about use of funds, restricted gifts, recurring donations, tax treatment, fees, refunds, and receipts. Name the person who approves those statements. An outside design team can clarify the journey, but it should not create fundraising or legal policy from examples found on other sites.

Identify the primary donor situations: someone responding to an urgent campaign, a returning supporter making a routine gift, a person donating in memory of someone, and a donor who needs help before completing payment. Not every organization needs every path. Select representative journeys and define their completion evidence so the scope stays tied to real operations.

## Choose amounts without manipulation

Suggested amounts can reduce decision effort, but they should reflect actual fundraising strategy and present a clear custom option. Explain whether examples of impact are illustrative or guaranteed. If a recurring option is emphasized, label frequency beside the amount throughout entry, review, confirmation, and receipt. A visual toggle that changes the financial commitment must be obvious to keyboard and screen-reader users as well as sighted pointer users.

Do not preselect extras, hide fees, or make the one-time choice difficult to find. Where the donor may cover transaction costs, explain the calculation and include it in the total before submission. Record who owns the wording and how changes will be reviewed across campaigns.

## Minimize the information requested

Ask only for information needed to process the gift, provide the selected acknowledgement, meet obligations, and support the donor. Separate required payment fields from optional communication preferences. Do not bundle marketing consent into the act of donating. State how tribute information, public recognition choices, employer matching details, and messages will be used.

For every field, record purpose, required status, destination system, retention owner, and error behavior. Use appropriate autocomplete attributes and input types where they help. W3C's [forms tutorial](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/forms/) explains labels, instructions, validation, and notifications; test the actual provider-hosted fields because embedded payment controls can behave differently from the surrounding page.

## Design for payment uncertainty

Map website, payment-provider, and fundraising-record states separately. A browser can lose connection after the provider accepts payment. A webhook can arrive late. A repeated click can create a second attempt. Define how the page checks status, prevents unsafe repeats, and tells a donor when confirmation is still pending. Never present a failed-looking screen if the organization cannot yet determine whether money moved.

Test declined payment, abandoned provider redirects, slow confirmation, duplicate submission, unsupported payment method, recurring setup failure, and receipt delay. Every message should offer a safe next step and a company-controlled support route. Logs and alerts may contain transaction identifiers but should avoid unnecessary cardholder or personal data.

## Keep trust evidence specific

Place the organization's identity, secure payment explanation, privacy link, contact route, and relevant registration details where donors can evaluate them. Avoid decorative trust badges with unclear meaning. Campaign stories and images need permission, respectful alternatives, and an owner who can remove or update them. Do not imply that a third-party payment logo endorses the organization.

Confirmation should restate amount and frequency, provide a useful reference, explain receipt delivery, and set expectations for account or campaign updates. It should not expose personal details in a shareable URL. For recurring gifts, include a clear route to manage or cancel according to approved policy.

## Measure the funnel without losing the donor

Define events for viewing the form, selecting frequency, encountering validation, handing off to a provider, returning, and reaching confirmed completion. Do not treat a button click as a donation. Document consent and data boundaries for analytics, especially where embedded payment components or cross-domain redirects are involved. Give event names and meanings to both the web team and fundraising analyst.

Accept the journey with test-mode transactions and a reconciliation check in the fundraising system. Confirm receipt ownership, refund handling, accessible error recovery, narrow-screen behavior, and what staff see when an integration fails. The handoff should let the nonprofit operate the journey without relying on the former design vendor for routine changes or donor support.

## Plan campaigns without forking the donation system

Define which parts of a donation journey a campaign editor may change: heading, story, image, suggested amounts, designation, appeal code, and return content. Keep payment behavior, required disclosures, consent, accessibility, receipt logic, and reconciliation under controlled ownership. This allows timely fundraising work without creating a separate untested form for every appeal.

Create a preview that shows the exact campaign, frequency, currency, and designation sent to downstream systems. Test expired and mistyped campaign links. Decide whether the visitor sees a current general-giving option, an explanation, or a closed page; never silently assign a gift to a different purpose when that would contradict the donor's intent. Track source with stable internal identifiers rather than putting personal details into URLs.

After a campaign, review more than conversion. Inspect payment exceptions, support contacts, refunds, recurring cancellations, unmatched records, accessibility feedback, and whether receipts carried the correct organizational details. Archive or update campaign content and remove claims that no longer apply. This operational loop gives the next outsourced production cycle real evidence instead of encouraging a fresh visual treatment built on the same unresolved problems.
