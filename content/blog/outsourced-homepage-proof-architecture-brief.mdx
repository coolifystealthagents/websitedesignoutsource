# Brief the Proof Architecture of an Outsourced Homepage

## A homepage is an argument, not a pile of modules

When an outside team receives a homepage brief made of section names—hero, logos, features, testimonials, FAQ—it can produce an orderly page that never answers the buyer's real questions. Begin with the decision a qualified visitor is trying to make. State what the organization offers, who it is for, the situation that makes it useful, and the next reasonable action. Then list the doubts that stop that action. The page structure should connect claims to evidence in the order a visitor needs them.

Call this the proof architecture. It is different from a wireframe. A wireframe describes placement; proof architecture describes why each claim deserves space and what substantiates it. The owner of a statement, its source, the audience it matters to, and its review status belong in the brief. The design team should never have to infer a credential, customer result, market position, or service boundary from a sales conversation.

## Inventory claims before writing headlines

Create a claim register with four columns: proposed claim, intended reader, approved evidence, and decision owner. Mark unsupported claims as questions, not copy. Evidence might be a verifiable certification, a documented process, an actual product capability, a customer-approved case study, or a precise description of what the team delivers. Decorative statistics and anonymous praise are not interchangeable with evidence.

Distinguish factual claims from positioning. "Next.js development" can be checked against service capability. "The easiest partner" is a comparative promise that needs a defensible basis and may still be unhelpful. A narrower statement such as "source files and review notes remain in your workspace" explains an operating benefit a prospect can evaluate. The copywriter and designer can make it clear and memorable after the company approves its truth.

## Map proof to moments of doubt

A visitor who has just arrived asks whether the offer is relevant. Near the opening, use a concrete service description and a recognizable work situation. Farther down, the visitor may ask what actually happens, whether the team can work with an existing stack, and whether handoff will be painful. Place process evidence, representative deliverables, and ownership commitments where those doubts arise. Do not force every visitor to cross a wall of logos before learning what is being sold.

Choose one primary action and one lower-commitment alternative. A ready buyer may contact the team; an earlier-stage buyer may inspect services or read a practical guide. Each call to action should say what comes next. "Get started" hides the commitment. "Describe the pages in your queue" gives the prospect a useful expectation and gives the receiving team context.

## Design the page for absent evidence

Sometimes the organization does not yet have permission to name customers, quantified outcomes, or mature case studies. The answer is not to fabricate social proof. Use evidence the company truly controls: an annotated sample deliverable, the review process, clear role boundaries, source ownership, accessibility practices, or a detailed example labelled as an example. State limitations honestly.

Give the designer rules for pending material. A missing testimonial should not create an empty carousel, and an unapproved metric should not appear in a launch build. Define fallback modules that still help the visitor decide. If a proof item expires or permission is withdrawn, the content model should allow removal without breaking the page's argument.

## Review comprehension before visual preference

Test a text-first outline with people who resemble the intended buyer. After a short read, ask what the company does, who it helps, what makes the offer credible, what is still unclear, and what action they expect after the primary button. Do not prompt them with the intended answers. Confusion at this stage is cheaper to correct than confusion hidden inside a polished prototype.

During visual review, trace every prominent element back to the proof architecture. If a large animation consumes attention, identify the decision it helps. If a card repeats a claim without evidence, combine or remove it. Check heading order, link purpose, zoom and reflow, motion preferences, image alternatives, and whether proof remains understandable without decorative media. W3C's [Web Content Accessibility Guidelines](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) provide the current accessibility criteria; they do not replace testing the specific argument with real content.

## Leave an evidence maintenance record

For every proof item, record its approver, source, publication permission, affected routes, and review trigger. Triggers can include a service change, certification expiry, customer request, new market, or revised legal position. Assign ownership to a company role rather than the temporary agency. The outside team can implement the module, but the business must own whether the claim remains true.

The accepted homepage package should include the claim register, content outline, final source copy, component mapping, responsive states, accessibility notes, analytics requirements, and a list of deliberately excluded claims. That package lets a future editor update the page without rebuilding its logic from screenshots.

## Use a proof-to-action review table

Before signoff, give each major page section a row containing the visitor question, the claim being made, the evidence shown, the intended next action, and the content owner. Read the rows without looking at the design. If the argument only makes sense because a reviewer remembers a sales presentation, the public page is missing context. If three sections answer the same question with weaker versions of the same claim, consolidate them and return attention to an unresolved objection.

Then inspect the rendered page at narrow, medium, and wide widths. Confirm that evidence remains beside the claim it supports, buttons retain specific labels, and the reading order still forms a coherent case. Disable images and test whether essential meaning survives. Review shared previews and metadata, because a title or social card can make a broader promise than the approved page copy.

Finally, ask the receiving marketing owner to update one proof item in a non-production environment. That exercise should reveal its source, permission status, affected components, preview route, and approval path. A homepage is ready for handoff when its argument can be maintained without asking the original designer to explain why each section exists.
