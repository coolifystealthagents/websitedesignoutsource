# Content Governance Evidence for Outsourced Websites

Content governance is the set of decisions that keeps a page accurate after publication. It is different from a style guide. A style guide can describe voice and formatting; governance identifies who owns a claim, how a change is requested, what evidence supports it, and when the page should be reviewed. This distinction matters when an external team contributes to a site that remains the company’s responsibility.

## Record ownership by content type

Assign an owner for service facts, legal text, accessibility statements, technical instructions, and editorial interpretation. One person may own several categories, but the record should still name the category. A designer should not silently approve a legal claim, and a writer should not infer a product promise from a visual mockup. The owner field is a control against accidental authority.

Google Search Central recommends keeping information accurate and useful for people. W3C guidance adds accessibility requirements for content structure and relationships. Together, they support a review record that includes the claim, source, route, date checked, reviewer, and disposition. The record should preserve rejected suggestions when the reason will help future reviewers.

## Change evidence

Each requested change should identify the current text, proposed text, reason, affected route, and acceptance condition. If the change alters an internal link or canonical, include those fields too. A broad request such as “make it clearer” needs a testable interpretation before production. The owner can choose a wording preference, but the record should distinguish preference from evidence.

## Review intervals and limits

Review timing depends on the content’s risk and rate of change. A legal notice, service scope, and evergreen explanatory page do not share one interval. Avoid inventing a universal schedule. Use a review date as a prompt, then confirm whether the source remains current. Governance can reduce ambiguity, but it cannot prove that a statement is true without a suitable source.

## Methodology

This article synthesizes W3C accessibility guidance, Google Search Central, Schema.org, and NIST risk concepts. It is a practical ownership model, not legal advice or a promise of search performance.

## Match control to the claim

Not every sentence needs the same governance burden. A navigation label may need an editorial review, while a statement about a regulated service, security practice, or measurable outcome needs an owner with authority to verify it. Classify a claim by consequence and change rate, then choose a proportionate record. The record can be small: claim text, route, source, checked date, owner, and decision. The important point is that the level of review follows the risk of being wrong rather than the length of the sentence.

Governance should also distinguish a content correction from a positioning change. Correcting a broken link has a different evidence path from changing “supports” to “guarantees.” The first is a technical repair with a direct test. The second changes the reader’s expectation and needs a factual owner. If both are placed in one undifferentiated queue, the easy edit may be completed while the consequential wording change receives only stylistic review.

## Preserve the history that explains a choice

A current page tells a future reviewer what is live, but not why a tempting alternative was rejected. Preserve material decisions when their rationale will prevent repetition: a source was outside scope, an example was removed because it could be mistaken for a client result, or a route was kept separate because the audience question differed. The record need not become a transcript. A concise reason, decision owner, and date are enough to give later changes context.

When a source expires or changes, do not silently update every dependent sentence. Identify the affected claims, assess whether the new source supports the same scope, and record the result. A changed statistic may require a new period, unit, or qualification. A changed standard may require a fresh interpretation. This approach keeps the page accurate without pretending that content maintenance is a mechanical text-substitution operation.

## A small-team operating model

For a small company, governance can use three roles even if one person fills more than one: a content owner who confirms facts, an editor who checks clarity and scope, and a publisher who verifies the approved artifact. The separation is about decisions, not headcount. If one person performs all three functions, the record should still show which question was answered at each stage. That makes delegation easier later and reduces the chance that an external contributor is treated as the authority for a company fact.

## Key Stats

When a page is revised, compare the proposed claim with the previous claim and the current source. This catches scope drift that a spelling review will miss. A new adjective can imply a guarantee; a removed qualifier can broaden a statement; a changed date can invalidate a comparison. The governance record should call out these meaning changes even when the markup diff is small.

Clear records make delegation safer. An external contributor can prepare a revision, but the named owner can decide whether the factual statement is acceptable. The page then carries both readable copy and a defensible reason for its final scope.

The smallest durable governance record has five questions: what changed, why, who owns the fact, what source was checked, and what remains uncertain. Adding a route and date makes the record discoverable. This is enough to support routine maintenance without requiring a large committee or a formal risk score for every sentence.

## Build a proportionate review path

Governance works when contributors know where a decision belongs. A content request can enter with a route, current wording, proposed wording, reason, evidence, and requested date. The owner then decides whether the change is factual, editorial, accessibility-related, or technical. Each category can have a different reviewer. The purpose is not bureaucracy; it is to prevent a person with authority over layout from silently becoming the authority over business claims.

Use a dependency view for material pages. If a service claim appears in a page title, navigation label, article, and structured description, list those uses before changing it. A correction that updates one occurrence but leaves another can create contradictory public meaning. Conversely, not every stylistic edit needs a site-wide review. Mapping the affected surfaces lets the owner choose a focused change and a focused validation.

Governance records should preserve negative decisions. If a proposed statistic is rejected because its period is unclear, record that reason. If a client example is held because publication permission is missing, record the hold. A later contributor can then seek the missing evidence instead of reintroducing the same unsupported material. Rejections also reveal recurring source gaps that may deserve a future evidence-gathering task.

Review intervals should be signals, not automatic truth. A page about a stable concept may need less frequent factual review than a page describing a changing service, but both can become inaccurate after an operational change. When the review prompt arrives, check the source and scope rather than simply renewing the prior approval. If the source cannot be checked, narrow or hold the claim. Governance improves reliability by showing that this decision was made; it cannot make an unverified statement true.

## Key Takeaways

- Name an owner for each high-risk content category.
- Preserve source, date, and decision evidence with every material change.
- Separate style preference from a factual or accessibility requirement.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does governance require a large committee?

No. It requires clear ownership and a repeatable decision record. A small team can use the same fields with fewer reviewers.

### Should every sentence have a citation?

No. Claims that affect trust, scope, compliance, or decisions need traceable support. Ordinary transitions do not.
