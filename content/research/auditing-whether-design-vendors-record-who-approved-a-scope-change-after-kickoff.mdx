## Research question and scope

This audit asks whether an outsourced design vendor keeps a record that names the person who approved a scope change after project kickoff. The unit of analysis is the approved change event, such as an added page template, a new language, a revised component set, or a shifted launch date that alters the agreed work. A qualifying record links the original commitment, the requested change, the named approver, the decision date, and the revised commitment. The review covers the period from kickoff to the first post-launch invoice.

The scope includes changes that affect cost, schedule, or deliverable count, whether or not the vendor billed for them. It excludes routine design revisions inside an already approved page, because those are covered by a different review and do not change the contract baseline. The audit does not judge whether a change was wise or fairly priced. It tests whether the vendor can show, months later, who agreed to move the boundary and when.

## Why scope approvals are easy to lose after kickoff

Scope changes usually arrive in the middle of a conversation rather than through a formal request form. A client asks for an extra landing page on a call, the designer says it is possible, and the work begins before anyone adjusts the plan. The verbal exchange feels like agreement to everyone present, yet it rarely names a version, a price, or a person with authority. By the time the invoice arrives, the paper trail depends on memory.

The problem compounds because the request, the estimate, and the approval often live in three different tools. A request may sit in email, an estimate in a spreadsheet, and the approval in a chat thread. Each artifact is reasonable on its own, but no single place shows the full decision. An auditor who searches only the project tracker will miss approvals that happened elsewhere.

## What counts as a record of a scope decision

A usable record has five parts: the baseline, the requested delta, the impact, the approver, and the effective date. The baseline can be the signed statement of work, an approved page list, or a versioned estimate. The delta should describe what is being added, removed, or moved. The impact should state the effect on cost, schedule, or both, even when the answer is "no additional fee."

Write the five parts into the change template so a record can be scored without interpretation. Each part should be findable in one place or linked from a single index entry. The approver is the part this audit cares about most. The record should name a person and a role, not a department or a distribution list. It should also show that the person had authority for that class of change, which may mean a named budget holder rather than the day-to-day client contact. A decision that says only "client approved" is weak because it cannot be matched to a person or a limit.

## Sampling scope changes from the change log

Begin with the vendor's change log or, where none exists, the tickets and messages that mention scope, estimate, or additional work. Search for terms such as change order, out of scope, additional page, extra round, revised estimate, and new deadline. Keep a record of the search terms and the date range so a second reviewer can repeat the work. Build a list of candidate changes before deciding which ones qualify.

Then filter the list against the baseline. A candidate qualifies when it moves a deliverable, a date, or a cost away from the approved plan. Changes that only clarify wording or correct a typo do not qualify. For a large project, stratify the sample by change type and by size, and include at least a few small changes, because informal approvals tend to hide in low-value work.

## Reading the approval thread

For each sampled change, pull the full thread that contains the decision rather than the single most recent message. Identify the message that requests the change, the message that states the impact, and the message that accepts it. Check whether those messages refer to the same version of the estimate, since a reply to an earlier number can look like approval of the current one. Quote the decisive messages in the audit file with their timestamps.

The order of events matters as much as the content. A vendor who starts work before approval may still have a valid record, but the audit should note the sequence because it changes the risk. A client who approves after the work is finished has ratified a decision rather than authorized one. Those cases are scored differently, and the difference should be visible in the report.

## Checking the baseline and the delta

Compare the approved change against the current statement of work, page list, or schedule. The revised baseline should reflect the change, and the change log should point to the version that contains it. If the two documents disagree, the auditor should record which one the team actually worked from. A change that was approved but never merged into the baseline will resurface as a billing dispute.

The delta deserves its own check because a vague scope change is hard to verify later. Ask whether the record states the number of added pages, the extra rounds, or the new date precisely enough for a reader to confirm delivery. "A few more pages" is not a testable commitment. A record that names three pages and a date can be checked against the built site and the launch record.

## Scoring the record set

Score each change on a four-level scale. Complete means all five parts are present and the revised baseline reflects the change. Partial means the approval is clear but one element, usually the impact or the effective date, is missing. Informal means the decision exists only in conversation or a chat message with no version reference. Missing means no approval can be found at all.

Report the counts by change type and by size so patterns are visible. If small changes are mostly informal while large ones are complete, the vendor may be applying a threshold that is not written down. That pattern calls for a defined fast path for small changes rather than a general instruction to document everything. The score is a description of the record, not a verdict on the vendor's honesty.

## Distinguishing approval from notification

A common confusion in these audits is treating a notification as an approval. A vendor who sends a message saying "we will add the page and it will cost the same" has informed the client, but may not have received a decision. The audit should look for an explicit acceptance, such as "approved," "go ahead," or a signed change order. Silence after a notification is not approval, however long it lasts.

The mirror error is treating a request as an approval. A client who asks whether an extra page is possible has not necessarily authorized it. The record should show the vendor's response with an impact and the client's subsequent acceptance. When the thread stops at the question, the change is unresolved even if the work later appears on the site.

## Common failure patterns

The most common pattern is a change that was approved in a meeting and never written down. Meeting notes may mention the decision, but they rarely name the approver's authority or the revised cost. A second pattern is an approval given by a contact who lacked budget authority, which becomes visible only when finance rejects the invoice. Both patterns produce a record that looks complete until someone checks the role.

A third pattern is a chain of small changes that were each treated as free. Individually they seem trivial, but together they move the scope far from the baseline. A fourth is a change approved after the work was delivered, which turns the record into a ratification. The audit should count these patterns separately so the remedy matches the cause, whether that is a written threshold, a named approver, or a stricter start-work rule.

## Limitations and threats to validity

This method reviews artifacts and cannot see everything that happened. A vendor may have obtained clear verbal approval that was never recorded, and a tidy record may describe a decision that was rushed or poorly understood. The auditor should corroborate the record with invoices, meeting notes, and the client's own files where permission exists. The report should name the sources it could not access instead of treating their absence as proof of a problem.

Retention is a further limit. Chat platforms expire messages, staff leave, and shared drives are reorganized, so the available record may be thinner than the original process. A short retention window can make a sound approval practice look incomplete. The audit should state the observation period and the tools available for each sample, and it should avoid generalizing from one project to a vendor's whole practice.

## Reporting and next action

Report one row per sampled change with the baseline reference, the delta, the impact, the approver, the decision date, and the revised baseline location. Mark each row complete, partial, informal, or missing, and add a short note for every gap. Give the vendor a defined window to supply missing records, then recheck a sample rather than the full set. The recheck shows whether the fix became a habit or only cleaned up the audit file.

The [NIST SP 800-53](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/53/r5/upd1/final) control catalog treats audit records and configuration control as related practices, which is a useful frame for tying an approval to a version. The [NIST SP 800-128](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/128/final) guide explains why a recorded baseline matters when many people can change a system. The [CISA Cyber Essentials](https://www.cisa.gov/cyber-essentials) offers a plain-language baseline for access and change control. These references inform the method; they do not report a finding for any vendor in this sample.
