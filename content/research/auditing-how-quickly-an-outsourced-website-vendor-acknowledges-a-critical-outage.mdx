## Research question and scope

A critical outage begins with a customer or monitoring alert and ends with a restored service. Between those points sits an acknowledgement, the moment a vendor confirms it has seen the report and is taking ownership. This study asks a narrow question: how long does that acknowledgement take, and what does the written record show about the path it followed? The scope is the incident history from one outsourced website maintenance relationship over twelve months.

The unit of analysis is one incident report and its first vendor acknowledgement. An incident qualifies when it affected a public page, checkout, or form for more than five minutes and was reported through the agreed channel. Routine content requests and planned maintenance windows are excluded. The method measures the written record, not the private intentions of the team.

## Why acknowledgement time is the first useful signal

Resolution time depends on the defect, the access, and sometimes a third party. Acknowledgement depends mostly on the vendor's process, which makes it a cleaner measure of responsiveness. A team that confirms receipt within minutes may still take hours to fix a complex fault. A team that never confirms receipt leaves the client guessing whether anyone is working. The first number is about ownership, the second is about repair.

NIST's incident response guidance treats detection and analysis as distinct from containment and recovery, which supports measuring the handoff between them. The acknowledgement is the visible handoff in a small maintenance relationship, and it depends on [monitoring that routes an alert to a person](/research/website-synthetic-monitoring-maintenance-handoff). It tells the client that the report entered a queue with an owner. Without it, the client keeps escalating and the vendor keeps losing time to duplicate messages.

## Defining acknowledgement precisely

Code an acknowledgement only when the message meets three conditions: a named person accepts ownership, the message arrives in the agreed channel, and it includes a next step or a question. An automated ticket receipt does not qualify, because it proves delivery rather than attention. A reply that says only thanks, we will look, is partial. Write the coding rule down before reading the incidents so the definition does not drift.

Separate the first human response from the first substantive update. The first human response is the acknowledgement, while the substantive update names a cause, a workaround, or a time estimate. Both matter, but they answer different questions. Record them as two timestamps so a fast hello cannot mask a slow diagnosis. This distinction keeps the measure honest when a vendor is polite but not yet useful.

## Building the incident sample

Start with every incident that met the threshold during the observation period, not only the ones that felt serious. Pull from the support inbox, the monitoring alerts, and the client's own messages, then deduplicate by time and affected page. Keep the original message and the full reply thread. A sample built only from the vendor's ticket system will miss reports that arrived by chat or phone.

Record the channel, the reporter, the affected service, the first alert time, the first human reply, and the restoration time. Note any message that went to a shared inbox without a named recipient. Exclude incidents where the client delayed sending information the vendor had already requested, but keep a note so the exclusion can be reviewed. Transparency about exclusions matters more than a larger sample.

## Choosing timestamps that survive review

Use the timestamp on the original message, not the time it was copied into a report. Time zones cause most of the errors, so convert every value to a single zone and keep the original beside it. A message sent at 23:50 and answered at 00:10 is a twenty-minute gap, not a next-day failure. Store the raw timestamps and the converted values in separate columns.

Where a channel lacks reliable timestamps, mark the record as estimated and exclude it from the headline figure. Do not reconstruct a time from memory or from a screenshot taken later. A small clean sample is stronger than a large sample with invented precision. Report the number of excluded records beside the result so the reader can judge the coverage.

## Coding acknowledgement paths

Classify each acknowledgement by the path it took: direct to a named engineer, into a shared queue, through an account manager, or not at all. The path explains the delay better than the duration alone. A queue that is checked twice a day will produce a consistent lag that no amount of apology will fix. A direct path may be fast but fragile when the named person is away.

Count how many incidents needed a second message from the client before anyone replied. That count is a strong indicator of a broken intake, because it measures repeated effort rather than a single slow response. Pair it with the channel used, since a phone call and a portal form behave differently, and check the [escalation log](/blog/wdo-escalation-log) when a report goes unanswered. The combination shows where the process fails, not just how often.

## Separating acknowledgement from resolution

A fast acknowledgement is not a fast repair, and conflating them lets two different problems hide. Report the median acknowledgement time and the median restoration time as separate figures with their own sample counts. If acknowledgement is fast and restoration is slow, the intake works and the repair capacity does not. If both are slow, the problem starts at the front door.

Also record how many incidents were acknowledged but never updated before restoration. A silent repair leaves the client unable to answer their own customers, which is a communication failure even when the fix is sound. Ask whether the vendor sent a closure note with a cause and a prevention step. The closure note is part of the service, not an optional courtesy.

## What the timestamps cannot tell you

Timestamps show when messages moved, not whether the right person was working or whether the fix was difficult. A vendor can acknowledge quickly and still misdiagnose for hours. A slow acknowledgement can reflect a genuine after-hours gap rather than neglect. Read the numbers beside the incident notes so the context stays attached to the measure. The goal is a better conversation, not a single score.

The record also favours written channels, because a phone call leaves no timestamp unless someone logs it. If most urgent reports arrive by phone, the sample will undercount the real response. Note the channel mix in the report and avoid comparing periods with different mixes. A measure that ignores its own blind spots becomes a target rather than a tool.

## Limitations and threats to validity

The sample comes from one relationship and one year, so it describes that relationship rather than the market. Seasonality, staff changes, and a single large migration can all shift the result. The coding also depends on how carefully the threads were preserved, and a deleted message cannot be recovered. Treat the finding as a baseline for this contract, not a benchmark for the industry.

Two reviewers should code a sample of incidents independently and compare results before the full pass. Disagreements usually reveal a vague definition, such as whether an emoji reply counts as acknowledgement. Resolve the definition, recode the sample, and record the change. This step is slow, but it prevents a confident number built on inconsistent judgment.

## Reporting and next action

Summarize the result in one page with the sample size, the median and range for acknowledgement, the share that needed a second client message, and the channel mix. Add one incident that illustrates the typical path and one that shows the worst delay. Name the cause you can act on, such as an unmonitored inbox or an unclear after-hours route. Keep the raw table beside the summary for anyone who wants the detail.

Agree one change with the vendor and a date to recheck it. A shared inbox with a named on-call owner is a common first fix, as is a rule that every critical report gets a human reply within a stated window. Repeat the measurement after two months with the same definitions. Improvement is credible only when the method stays fixed while the process changes.
