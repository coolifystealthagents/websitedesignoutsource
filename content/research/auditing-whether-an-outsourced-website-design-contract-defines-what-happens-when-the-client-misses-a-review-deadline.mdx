## Research question and scope

This audit asks whether an outsourced website design contract states what happens when the client misses a scheduled review deadline. The unit of analysis is the contract, statement of work, review schedule, and the written notices exchanged during a live project. The review covers design approvals, content approvals, staging sign-off, and launch authorization, because each stage can carry a different deadline and a different consequence. Every clause is quoted with its location so the finding can be checked by another reader.

The scope includes the trigger that starts the review window, the method by which the client receives the material, and the remedy available to either party when no response arrives. It also includes any clause that treats silence as approval, extends the schedule, or allows the vendor to invoice for standby time. The audit does not offer legal advice and does not decide whether a clause is enforceable. It tests whether the contract gives both parties a predictable process before a deadline is missed.

## Why silence is a contract term, not an absence of one

A contract that says nothing about a missed review does not leave the parties free of consequences. It leaves them to negotiate after the schedule has slipped, when the vendor has staff idle and the client feels blamed. The vendor may treat silence as approval and continue, or it may pause and later claim a change order. Either response can be reasonable, which is exactly why the missing term becomes a dispute.

A written rule also protects the client. If silence is automatically approval, the client needs to know when the clock starts and how to stop it. If silence pauses the project, the client needs to know what notice the vendor must send and whether fees continue. The audit looks for that rule rather than assuming one side's customary practice. A contract that names the consequence is easier to administer than a relationship that relies on goodwill.

## Defining the audit fields

The audit uses eight fields: review stage, trigger event, delivery method, response window, reminder notice, default outcome, schedule effect, and fee effect. The review stage names the milestone being approved, such as a homepage concept or a launch checklist. The trigger event states whether the window begins when the vendor sends the file, when the client opens it, or when a meeting ends. The delivery method records the channel and the required recipient.

The remaining fields describe what happens after the window closes. The response window gives the number of business days and the time zone. The reminder notice states whether the vendor must send a second message before the default takes effect. The default outcome says whether silence means approval, rejection, or a pause. The schedule and fee effects explain the practical consequence, including any right to reallocate staff or charge a restart fee.

## Locating the review clause and its definitions

Search the contract for review, approval, acceptance, sign-off, deadline, and deemed. Repeat the search in the statement of work, the project plan, and the change order template, because a consequence is sometimes placed in an appendix rather than the main agreement. Record the section number and the exact sentence for each hit. A clause that exists only in an email thread is not part of the signed contract unless the agreement incorporates it.

Then read the definitions that the clause depends on. "Business day" may exclude holidays in one party's country but not the other. "Deliverable" may include a clickable prototype in the statement of work but only a static image in the contract. "Approval" may require a named signatory or may accept a project manager's email. The audit records these definitions beside the deadline rule so the trigger can be applied consistently.

## Checking the notice and delivery method

A deadline is only usable if both parties can prove when the review material arrived. The contract should name the delivery channel, the recipient, and the time at which the message is treated as received. Sending a file to a shared drive without a notification is weaker evidence than an email with a link and a read receipt. The audit records which method the vendor actually used during the sample project.

Look for a required reminder before any default outcome. A single message that disappears into a busy inbox should not trigger automatic approval of a major design decision. A useful clause requires a second notice with a clear subject line and a stated deadline. It may also require the vendor to offer a short call before the window closes. The audit marks the reminder as present, absent, or discretionary based on the contract language.

## Testing the deemed-acceptance trigger

If the contract treats silence as approval, identify the exact moment approval occurs. Is it the end of the response window, the end of the next business day, or the start of the vendor's next production cycle? Check whether the clause applies to every deliverable or only to minor revisions. A deemed-acceptance rule that covers a full redesign is a different risk from one that covers a spelling correction.

Then check whether the client has a way to interrupt the trigger. A client who sends a partial comment should not be treated as silent, and a client who asks for a short extension should get a documented answer. The contract should say whether a partial response pauses the clock or counts as a rejection. The audit records the ambiguity because it is likely to surface in the first busy review cycle.

## Reading the consequences of a missed deadline

List what the vendor may do after the deadline passes. Common options include proceeding to the next stage, pausing the project, releasing reserved capacity, or charging a restart fee. For each option, record the notice the vendor must give and the evidence it must keep. A clause that allows a fee without a notice requirement gives the client little chance to respond before the invoice arrives.

Then list what the client may do. Can the client request an extension, reopen an approval, or require a new review before launch? Does a missed deadline waive any acceptance right, and does it affect the warranty period? The audit should show whether the consequence is proportionate to the stage. A missed comment on a mood board should not produce the same result as a missed launch authorization.

## Comparing the clause with the actual review calendar

Read the project schedule and count the review windows it promises. Compare each window with the contract's definition of a business day and with the holiday calendar for both parties. A schedule that gives the client three days over a public holiday is shorter than it looks. The audit records the effective window for each milestone rather than accepting the headline number.

Then compare the schedule with the notices actually sent. If the vendor delivered a package late but kept the original deadline, the client lost review time through no fault of its own. If the vendor sent the package to the wrong contact, the trigger should not start. The audit should document these mismatches because they are the practical test of whether the clause works in the relationship.

## Evaluating change orders and fee protection

A missed review often becomes a change order rather than a clean default. Check whether the contract says how the vendor will price the delay and whether the client must approve the change order before work resumes. A clause that allows an open-ended standby charge is harder to budget than a fixed restart fee. The audit records the formula and any cap.

Also check the interaction between a missed deadline and a fixed fee. If the project is priced per stage, a delay may move revenue without changing the total. If the vendor bills hourly, the client may pay for time spent waiting as well as time spent working. The contract should state which model applies and how the vendor records the waiting time. Without that record, the invoice becomes an argument about memory.

## Reviewing the escalation path before silence

A deadline clause should sit beside an escalation path. The contract should name who the vendor contacts when a review is late, how quickly that person escalates, and what happens if the client's project sponsor is unavailable. A reminder sent only to a coordinator who is on leave does not give the client a fair chance to respond. The audit records the named roles rather than the individual names.

The escalation path should also cover the vendor's own delays. If the vendor misses the delivery date that starts the review window, the client should not lose review time. A symmetrical clause is easier to defend and easier to operate. The audit looks for that symmetry because a one-sided deadline rule tends to produce distrust even when the vendor never enforces it.

## Limitations and threats to validity

This audit reads documents and a sample of project correspondence. It cannot establish how a court would interpret an ambiguous clause, and it should not be presented as legal advice. The finding describes the process the contract creates, not the outcome of a dispute. A lawyer should review any clause the client intends to rely on in a high-value project.

The sample may also be too small to show how the vendor behaves across many clients. One project with a generous extension does not prove that the clause is never enforced, and one strict reminder does not prove a pattern. Keep the finding tied to the documents reviewed. If the client wants a stronger view, ask for the review logs from several recent projects and compare them.

## Reporting and next action

Report one row per review stage with the trigger, delivery method, window, reminder, default outcome, schedule effect, and fee effect. Quote the clause and attach the relevant schedule and notice examples. Mark every ambiguity and give it an owner, whether that is the vendor, the client's legal reviewer, or the project sponsor. Then agree the reminder template and the escalation contact before the next milestone.
